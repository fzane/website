You're staring at something you made with AI and feeling vaguely uncomfortable. Did you write this, or did you just... approve it?

The current discourse offers two positions: AI is a tool and you're still doing "real work," or you're becoming just a manager of AI agents — writing specs, delegating, reviewing. Neither quite captures what's actually happening. And neither answers the question that's actually bothering you: does your expertise still matter if AI is doing the tasks?

Both framings inherit a modern assumption: there's a sharp line between what bosses do (decide, direct, approve) and what workers do (execute, produce, deliver). But that's not the only way to organize expert work.

## The Workshop

Renaissance art workshops solved a version of this problem. The _bottega_ — literally "shop" — was the standard structure for producing art in 15th and 16th century Italy. Raphael's workshop in Rome is the famous example: at its peak, he employed dozens of assistants producing the frescoes that covered the Vatican's walls. The _School of Athens_ — the famous painting with Plato pointing up to the heavens and Aristotle gesturing down to the earth — was a workshop production. The master craftsman's name was on the wall, but supported by many hands.

His job wasn't just client relations and quality control. He created what was called the _cartoon_ — a full-scale preparatory drawing that guided execution. Think of it as a visual spec and task list dividing the work into sections, distributed across assistants who would transfer lines to wet plaster and fill in the paint.

But here's what we're missing in current AI discourse: the master also _remained a practicing craftsman_. He would step in for specific interventions — faces and hands, boundaries between sections, final glazes. Not because these were generically "the hard parts" but because these were where expert attention created disproportionate value. The master was both director and doer.

## The Expert Touch

When did the master shift from director to doer?

**Faces and hands.** These are what people look at and remember. A master could specify how a face should look in the cartoon, but the painting could succeed or fail on these details.

For written work, your faces and hands are things like the opening and the pivot. The first paragraph is where readers decide whether to keep reading. The pivot is where you turn from setup to insight — where the reader either checks out or decides to share the piece.

The reason these matter is that you have something the AI doesn't: a richer model of your specific audience. You know what they've heard before, what they're skeptical of, what will land. The AI has general patterns; you have _this_ context.

**Seams.** Different assistants working on different sections would have subtly different hands — variations in color, brushwork, interpretation. The master's job was integration: smoothing the boundaries where work met, ensuring the piece cohered as one vision.

AI-assisted writing has characteristic seams where sections meet. Between setup and argument, between evidence and interpretation, between one idea and the next. Each part might work; the transition is where things break.

Good transitions resist prompting. They depend too much on the specific context of what comes before and after. Sometimes the fastest path is just writing it yourself — the bridge sentence that makes the connection feel inevitable. That's craft knowledge: easier to do than to specify.

**Final glazes.** Renaissance painters applied thin, translucent layers of oil paint at the end to unify color temperature, adjust values, create atmospheric depth. This wasn't fixing mistakes. It was harmonization that could only happen after seeing the whole.

Your equivalent isn't copyediting. It's the work that can only happen after you see the whole: the title that crystallizes what the piece turned out to be about, the conclusion that lands differently now that you know what you're concluding. You couldn't specify these in advance. They're responses to what emerged.

## Running Your Bottega

Here's what changes if you take this seriously: you're not "using a tool." You're running a bottega.

The Renaissance master was a brand (the name that commanded premium), a production system (workshop capacity for execution at scale), a quality function (the eye that maintained standards), and a judgment engine (knowing what the work required). All bundled in one person, supported by assistants who handled execution.

That bundled structure is what AI hands you. You are a firm of one, even if you're an employee. What matters is the output — and you're judged on its quality, not on whether you personally executed every step. Brand, production capacity, quality function, judgment: all yours now, supported by execution capability that happens to be artificial.

A century later, Rubens made this explicit: his workshop had published pricing tiers based on how much he did: "by his own hand" versus "begun by a pupil." The market had vocabulary for expert attention and effort. We're currently in danger of flattening that into either "human-made" or "AI-generated," as if those were the only categories that make sense.

This come back to the original question: Did you write this or merely approve it?  Both.  

That is the double expertise we've been describing. The Raphael who created the _School of Athens_ designed the specification and architecture like a manager.  But he also painted the faces and smoothed the seams as a craftsman. Both were expressions of his expertise, even if the space between was filled by others.
