Types of Intelligence.

We built one kind of mind. You are another — and the difference is the most practical thing you can know about working with it. A continuous scroll; read it once, and the margin beside you will have written the summary.

AI feels like a verdict on what people are for. It isn't.

the engine took carrying writing took remembering the calculator took arithmetic the press took copying the spreadsheet took the ledgers AI takes — what, exactly? the open question

Every powerful tool resets the line between the work we keep and the work we hand off — and every time, it feels like a referendum on what we are. It never is. It's a negotiation. The unease you feel about AI is that negotiation happening, not a verdict coming in.

And the negotiation runs along the same line every time.

On one side, the work we can spell out — where we can say in advance exactly what done looks like. On the other, the work we can only recognize once we see it. Every tool we've ever made has eaten further into the first kind. AI just eats faster, and reaches higher up.

working definition

A large, centralized mind trained to pass every test we can think to write for it.

This was a choice, not a discovery. We didn't summon intelligence in the abstract — we picked one type to build: a mind shaped to clear our bars. Reading, code, math, anything we could score. It is, almost literally, our expectations made executable.

The technology changes. The role changes with it.

carry → ride → drive → navigate

For most of history, part of the job was carrying things over distances. Horses, then engines, ended that — and the job became deciding where to go. The role we take up beside a tool is never only about us. It's about the shape of the thing we built.

Our new role is contingent on the particular kind of mind we happened to build. We didn't get the hands, and we didn't get the grandmother. We got the third one — so build it a different way, and the whole story changes.

One giant mind.

a perfect lattice — every copy identical

It doesn't know one trick — it knows all of them: every skill we could write a test for, concentrated in a single thing we copy at enormous scale. Every copy identical. A monument built entirely out of the expected.

Now imagine the opposite design.

released — each its own size, its own life

A swarm of tiny minds, each trained on a different life. No single one knows everything; each knows its own corner deeply. An engineer can tell you what it's for: you build consensus when you know what you're looking for, and a diverse swarm when you don't.

↙ one of these is you

We never had to build that one. It's you.

Your life is its training run — a rollout no one else got. You're not the giant mind with less data in it. You're the other architecture entirely: the one made of everything that couldn't be written down. Yet.

Can you write down, in advance, a rubric for what a good result looks like? If yes — write it, hand it over, let it run. If you'll only know it's right once you see it, the work is yours — because you're the only instrument that can score it.

If you can write the rubric, it's no longer your job.

Living on the divide is a craft with two halves: you have to transmit a want to the machine — and, harder, form the want in the first place. Both are learnable. Here's how.

Transmitting a want: genre is the handle.

Then give the almost a shape.

Articulating is finding an external structure that can hold together a coherence that until now was only an internal felt sense.

That's the whole arc of forming intent: notice the thing only you can see, react to candidates until one almost fits, and articulate the one that holds. The moment you do, you've written the rubric — so hand it over.

Learn the expected well enough to recognize it — then stop, and hand it over.

Ask for probes, not products — your “not quite” is the one reading no instrument but you can take.

Every want you write down becomes a rubric the machine absorbs. That's the loop working, not the machine winning.
